00 · BEFORE WE KNEW

We didn't know
we were making memories.

We were just children. We wanted to get home before dark. We wanted to watch our favourite programme. We wanted the light to come back before the interesting part of the movie. We wanted enough money for a snack after school.

We complained about school. We complained about chores. We complained when Mummy said it was time to sleep. We complained when Daddy changed the channel.

We did not know that ordinary days were quietly becoming the days we would one day wish we could visit again.

01 · PRIMARY SCHOOL

Before school became a memory.

There was something about getting ready for school that felt completely ordinary then. Now, even the smallest parts of it feel precious.

The uniform was usually the first problem. It was either too big because somebody had bought a size that would “still fit next year,” or it was already becoming too tight because children apparently grew faster than school uniforms could handle.

The shirt had been ironed. The socks were pulled up. The shoes had been polished. The school badge sat proudly where it was supposed to.

Then came the bag. It carried exercise books, textbooks, ruler, pencil, eraser, sharpener, lunch and water. Sometimes it carried a note from the teacher that you desperately hoped would disappear before your parents saw it.

There were Macmillan textbooks, English books, Mathematics, Basic Science, Social Studies and all those books that somehow made a small child feel as if they were carrying an entire library.

And there were those serious morning lessons for Common Entrance and other examinations. You could be barely awake and somebody was already telling you that your future depended on solving another set of questions.

At the school gate, everybody arrived at once. Some children were still crying. Some were looking for their friends. Older pupils moved around with the confidence of people who owned the whole compound.

Then came assembly: prayers, anthem, announcements and a prefect shouting at somebody to stand properly.

And then, eventually, the bell.

Break time.

The classroom emptied as if somebody had opened a cage. Somebody had chin-chin. Somebody had biscuits. Somebody had brought something everybody wanted to taste.

After break came lessons, copying notes, answering questions, watching the clock and waiting for the teacher to finish.

Then the closing bell rang and suddenly the same heavy backpack felt light.

Nobody told us we would miss the noise, the uniforms, the blackboard, the school gate and the feeling that tomorrow would always come.

02 · HOME

“Who gave you permission?”

Our parents could turn one innocent afternoon into a full investigation.

There were rules everywhere. Greet properly. Finish your homework. Wash the plates. Don't touch the remote without asking. Don't go outside without telling somebody.

If an older relative entered the house, you were expected to stand up and greet them. And if you forgot, somebody would remind you.

Then there were the questions.

“Where are you going?”
“Who are you going with?”
“What time are you coming back?”
“Who gave you permission?”

Sometimes the most dangerous moment was hearing your full name from another room.

You could be perfectly innocent and still begin remembering everything you had done that day.

And then there was that famous command: “Omo, get inside!”

You could be outside with five other children, completely lost in the game. Somebody's mother had already called them. Another parent had called another child. Your own mother had probably called you once already.

You pretended not to hear.

Then the voice came again — louder, fuller, carrying your name across the compound.

At that point, the game was over.

But childhood was not always about staying indoors. Some parents allowed children to move freely between neighbours' houses. You could knock on somebody else's door and ask for your friend. Their mother knew you. Your mother knew their children. The compound could feel like one large extended family.

Report-card day had its own atmosphere. You knew the good subjects. You knew the ones that needed explanation. You might even have rehearsed your speech before arriving home.

Sometimes there was punishment. Sometimes there was a long lecture. Sometimes there was simply that disappointed silence that made you wish somebody would shout instead.

Looking back, many of those rules came from parents trying to teach responsibility in the way they knew. That does not make every method right, and childhood discipline could sometimes hurt. But underneath the strictness was often care: the insistence that you greet people, work hard, behave well and become somebody they could be proud of.

We thought they were making childhood difficult. We did not yet understand how much of their worry was love wearing a serious face.

03 · WHAT WE USED TO WATCH

The remote was power.

One television could hold an entire family hostage.

There was always somebody watching something. Cartoons in the afternoon. A movie later. Super Story. Papa Ajasco. Dear Mother. My Mum & I. Mount Zion. Old Nollywood.

The remote control was not merely a remote control. It was authority.

One person wanted cartoons. Another wanted the news. Somebody else was waiting for a football match. An older sibling had already announced that they were watching their programme.

Sometimes the argument was quiet. Sometimes it became a family meeting.

“Who changed it?”

“I was watching that!”

“Give me the remote.”

And then an adult entered the room.

Suddenly everybody became quiet. The adult took the remote, changed the channel and continued watching as though the argument had never happened.

But television gave us more than programmes. It gave us shared references. We repeated lines from movies. We copied characters. We argued about actors.

We watched the same film so many times that we knew what would happen before it happened.

The television was never just a screen. It was where siblings argued, where families gathered, and where ordinary evenings became part of the family story.

04 · WHEN NEPA TOOK THE LIGHT

“Up NEPA!”

There are phrases that belong to a particular generation. This one still sounds like childhood.

You could be sitting in front of the television, gripping your snack, completely invested in the story.

Kpa!

The screen went black.

“Up NEPA!” somebody shouted.

Then came the routine. Someone checked whether the neighbours still had power. Somebody looked for the rechargeable lamp. Someone started complaining. Someone said it would return soon.

At night, the darkness changed the house. The fan stopped. The room became warmer. Conversations lasted longer because there was suddenly nothing else to do.

And if the generator started, there was relief. You heard the sound from somewhere outside and immediately knew the evening had been rescued.

Sometimes electricity returned at the worst possible time. Sometimes it returned exactly when you had stopped expecting it.

We complained, of course. But those evenings taught us how to make something out of less. We talked. We played. We sat outside. We listened to the radio.

Today, darkness can feel like an inconvenience. Back then, it could become an entire evening of memories.

05 · COME OUTSIDE

Before the phone became the playground.

Somebody would shout from the compound, “Come outside!” and suddenly the whole afternoon belonged to everybody.

We had games that required almost nothing. A stone. A line drawn on the ground. A ball. A skipping rope. A few friends and enough imagination to turn an ordinary street into an entire world.

There was Suwé. Ten-Ten. Police and Thief. Hide and Seek. Football. Ayo. Ludo. Whot. Hand-clapping games.

Each one had rules, even if the rules changed whenever somebody was losing.

There were arguments too.

“It is my turn!”
“You cheated!”
“No, I didn't!”

Five minutes later everybody was playing again.

Football could begin with two slippers serving as goalposts. Ayo could attract serious concentration. Ten-Ten could turn a dusty patch of ground into a competition.

And then, eventually, somebody's mother would call their name.

“Come inside!”

You would pretend not to hear the first time.

Maybe the second.

But when the call came again, louder, you knew the afternoon was over.

We didn't need Wi-Fi to find people. We only needed to step outside.

06 · WHAT WE USED TO EAT

Small money.
Big happiness.

Childhood had a special relationship with small amounts of money.

You did not need much to feel rich. A little money after school could become a snack, a drink, something to share with a friend or something you carefully saved for tomorrow.

The school gate had its own economy. Gala. Goody Goody. Speedy. Okin. Robo Robo. Baba Dudu. Biscuits. Chin-chin. Small chops at parties.

You could stand there looking at everything, calculating what you could afford and what would leave enough for another day.

And food was rarely just food. Somebody bought something and five friends immediately became interested.

“Give me small.”
“Just one.”
“Let me taste it.”

Sometimes you shared willingly. Sometimes you guarded your snack like a security officer.

There were also the meals at home — foods that seemed ordinary because they appeared regularly, until you grew older and realised how much work went into making them.

We did not think of these moments as nostalgia. We were simply eating.

Yet years later, a familiar wrapper, smell or taste can bring back an entire afternoon.

Sometimes childhood happiness really was that simple: a snack, a friend and nowhere else you needed to be.

07 · THE BOARDING HOUSE

Then came JSS1.

Boarding school could feel like entering a jungle: new rules, unfamiliar faces, seniority, inspections, hostel life, small portions of food, half-bucket baths, social nights and the powerful relief of seeing home again on visiting day.

The first day arrived with luggage that looked as if you were moving permanently. A box. A bucket. A mattress. Provisions. Clothes. Shoes. Toiletries. School supplies.

Your parent helped you arrange everything. You tried to look brave.

Then came the goodbye.

You watched the car move away, and the compound suddenly looked bigger than it had five minutes earlier.

JSS1 felt like entering a jungle because you had to learn a completely new social world. Seniors knew things you did not know. There were rules that were not always written down.

Even ordinary things became skills. Bathing, for example, was not always an endless shower. Sometimes a half bucket had to be enough.

You learned how to use it carefully. You learned to bathe quickly. You learned that somebody else was waiting.

There was also the culture of rub and shine. Shoes had to look presentable. Uniforms had to be neat. Beds had to be arranged.

Food was another adjustment. Portions could feel small, especially when you were used to eating at home whenever you were hungry.

Provisions from home became valuable. A biscuit, drink or snack from your own box could suddenly feel like treasure.

The seniority system could be intimidating. Some seniors were kind. Some were strict. There were punishments and harsh experiences, and some students genuinely found boarding school difficult.

The hostel had its own folklore too. Stories about mysterious footsteps and figures like Madam Koi-Koi could travel through a dormitory faster than any official announcement.

Whether anybody truly believed the stories was almost beside the point. The storytelling itself became part of boarding-school culture.

Then came inter-house sports. The school that sometimes felt like a strict machine suddenly became loud and colourful.

And then there was social night. Music played. Students dressed up. People danced and laughed. For a few hours, school felt completely different.

But perhaps the most emotional day was visiting day.

Long before your parents arrived, you were already looking toward the gate. Every approaching car created a little hope.

Then you finally saw them.

Suddenly you remembered what home smelled like. Food from home. Drinks. Provisions. Familiar voices. Hugs. Questions. Complaints. Stories.

The hardest part was watching them leave again.

And then there was the freedom of leaving school during permitted outings, holidays or the journey home. Outside the gate, the world felt different. No prefect watching. No bell controlling every movement.

Boarding school was not one thing. It could be harsh, funny, frightening, lonely, exciting and deeply formative. At first it was a jungle. Later, the same place could become a second home.

08 · BEFORE EVERYTHING WAS SMART

When a phone was just a phone.

There was a time when being unreachable for several hours was normal.

Nokia. Sony Ericsson. Later BlackBerry and early Android phones. We knew phones by their shapes, ringtones, buttons and the person who owned them.

A phone could be a status symbol, a useful tool or simply the thing that allowed you to call home when you needed to.

The phone did not need to do everything. Calls were important. Text messages mattered. There was an alarm, calculator, contacts and maybe a few games.

Snake could keep somebody occupied for longer than it deserved. A good ringtone could make everybody turn their head.

Then came Bluetooth. Somebody had a song. Somebody else wanted it. The transfer began.

Then 2go, Facebook, BBM and early social media changed friendship. Suddenly you could talk to somebody who was not physically beside you.

But the phone still had limits. If you were bored, you could call somebody, send a message, listen to music or put the phone down and go outside.

We thought technology was making life easier. We did not know how much quieter life had once been.

09 · THE ZEE WORLD ERA

“Just one more episode.”

Then came the period when television drama became part of the daily routine.

There was a particular kind of evening when you knew exactly what you wanted to watch. You settled into the sitting room, found the remote and waited.

Zee World became a major part of television culture for many Nigerian viewers during this period.

And the titles became familiar names in everyday conversation: Saloni. Married Again. Silver Lining.

You did not necessarily watch because you had some deep analysis of television storytelling. You watched because the characters became part of the routine.

Somebody could walk into the room and ask, “What happened yesterday?”

And suddenly a ten-minute explanation became a full family discussion.

There were betrayals. Weddings. Family secrets. People separating and somehow finding their way back together. Characters making decisions that made everybody shout at the television.

And the funniest part was the promise: “I'll watch only this episode.”

One episode became another. Another became tomorrow's conversation.

For many people, that era is now tied to a particular sitting room, a particular television, a particular period of school or university, and the people who used to watch beside them.

Sometimes nostalgia is not about the programme itself. It is about remembering who was sitting beside you when it was on.

DECEMBER

10 · DECEMBER

December used to feel longer.

Maybe December did not actually last longer. Maybe childhood simply knew how to wait.

The final weeks of school carried a particular excitement. Everybody knew December was coming.

New clothes were being discussed. School parties were happening. Church programmes were appearing on the calendar. Some schools had end-of-year celebrations and Father Christmas.

Then the final bell came. School closed. Suddenly the calendar looked enormous.

No morning assembly. No rushing to prepare uniforms. No homework waiting because school was tomorrow.

Cousins arrived from different places and became your closest friends within an hour because childhood did not need introductions for very long.

New clothes were a serious event. You could remember what you wore on a particular Christmas years later.

There were church services, carols, family visits, decorations and meals that seemed to keep appearing.

And perhaps that was why December felt so long. There was less urgency. Tomorrow did not seem to be chasing you.

We thought Christmas was special because of the gifts and new clothes. Growing older taught us that the deeper gift was having people around the same table.

THEN WE GREW UP

11 · THEN WE GREW UP

We thought growing up meant getting taller.

Nobody warned us that one day we would miss the very things we once begged to escape.

At some point, school stopped being a place we complained about and became a place we remembered.

The uniform disappeared. The classroom changed. The morning assembly became a story.

At one time, twenty children could share a classroom and know almost everything about one another.

Who always had snacks. Who was always late. Who could run. Who could sing. Who was always in trouble.

Then life scattered everybody. Secondary school. University. Work. Marriage. Relocation. Different cities. Different countries.

Adulthood brought a different kind of pressure. Rent. Bills. Deadlines. Applications. Work. Career uncertainty. Responsibilities.

Then something small can take you backwards.

A song. The smell of a particular food. An old Nokia phone. A school uniform. A generator starting. A familiar television programme.

Suddenly you remember the child who could spend an entire afternoon outside with almost nothing.

We should not pretend childhood was perfect. It was not.

Some children experienced difficult homes, harsh discipline, loss, fear or circumstances they never deserved. Nostalgia should not erase those realities.

But neither should difficulty erase the ordinary beauty that existed alongside them: friendships, play, family, food, school, laughter, holidays and the feeling that there would always be another afternoon tomorrow.

We cannot return to those days. But memory gives us another kind of return.

For a moment, we can remember what it felt like to be there.

We used to be those children. And somehow, they are still here — in the things we laugh about, the people we miss, the songs we remember, the foods that taste like home and the small moments that still make us stop and smile.

We used to be those children.
We still carry them.
